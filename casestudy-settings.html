Spendflo SaaS Platform Information Architecture 2023

From Config Panel
to Control Hub

A complete redesign of Spendflo's Settings experience — restructuring a sprawling, hard-to-use configuration area into a purposeful, scalable control hub. Result: +37% settings adoption.

Timeline 6 Weeks
My Role End-to-End Designer
Key Result +37% Adoption
Platform Web Application

Context

Settings as a business problem

Spendflo is a SaaS spend management platform that helps companies manage vendor contracts, renewals, and software procurement. Settings in Spendflo isn't just a preferences page — it's the control layer for integrations, user permissions, approval workflows, and budget policies.

The original Settings page had grown organically over two years of feature additions. 47 individual settings were crammed into a single flat list with minimal grouping. Users — typically finance leads and IT admins — reported feeling "lost" and routinely opened support tickets for tasks they should have been able to self-serve.

Business Impact

The cost of poor discoverability

Low Settings adoption had a direct cost: unapplied configurations meant Spendflo wasn't fully activated in accounts, reducing the product's perceived value and increasing churn risk. Customer Success was spending 30% of onboarding time manually walking clients through Settings.

The redesign brief was framed not as "improve UX" but as "increase the proportion of accounts that reach full configuration within the first 30 days." This framing kept the design work focused on measurable business outcomes.

Diagnosis

What was broken and why

01

No mental model alignment

Settings were organised by when they were built, not by how users think about them. "Slack Notifications" was 20 items away from "Approval Workflows" — related concepts scattered across the page.

02

No progressive disclosure

Every setting was visible to every user, regardless of their role. A billing admin saw developer API tokens. An IT admin saw finance-only policies. The signal-to-noise ratio was poor for everyone.

03

No completion feedback

There was no way to know if settings were correctly configured. No status indicators, no "recommended next step" patterns, no onboarding completion state. Users couldn't tell if they were done.

Design Process

Six weeks, first principles

01

Usage Analytics + Session Recordings

Analysed Mixpanel data for which settings were most visited, most abandoned, and most frequently raised in support tickets. Watched 20 session recordings from Hotjar to observe actual navigation patterns. Found 12 settings were accessed weekly; 31 settings had near-zero engagement.

02

Role-Based Persona Mapping

Defined three primary admin personas: Finance Lead, IT Admin, and Operations Manager. Mapped which settings each persona cared about, their frequency of use, and their mental model for where those settings "lived." This directly informed the new IA.

03

Information Architecture Restructure

Consolidated 47 settings into 7 logical groups: Account, Users & Permissions, Integrations, Approvals, Budgets, Notifications, and Developer. Ran open card sorting with 6 power users to validate groupings. Achieved 87% agreement on primary groupings across sessions.

04

Progressive Disclosure + Setup Status

Introduced role-based visibility — settings irrelevant to a user's permissions are hidden, not just greyed out. Added a "Setup Checklist" module on the Settings homepage showing percentage completion and prioritised next steps. This alone drove the largest adoption jump in the first week post-launch.

05

Search + Quick Access

Added full-text search across all settings with instant results. Added a "Pinned Settings" feature so power users could surface their most-used controls. Reduced average navigation depth from 3.2 clicks to 1.4 clicks for common tasks.

Strategic Lens

Design as a retention lever

This project crystallised something I now apply across all my work: Settings adoption is a proxy for product activation. A product that isn't fully configured isn't fully used — and a product that isn't fully used churns. Framing the redesign as a retention problem gave it executive sponsorship it wouldn't have gotten as a "UX improvement."

The setup checklist module had the highest individual ROI of any single design decision. It cost half a day to design, half a sprint to build, and was the primary driver of the adoption lift. Sometimes the highest-value design is the simplest one.
