01  ·  Overview

What is a social media campaign?

A social media campaign is a coordinated marketing effort that uses social media platforms to achieve specific goals — building brand awareness, driving traffic, generating leads, or promoting a product. The complexity is in the coordination, not the concept.

🌐
Wide reach at scale

Billions of active users across platforms, with precise targeting by demographics, interest, and behaviour. Campaigns reach the right people at the right moment.

💬
Real-time feedback loops

Unlike broadcast channels, social media is a two-way medium. Users comment, share, and react — giving teams immediate signal to iterate their messaging.

📊
Measurable, adjustable

Every impression, click, and conversion is trackable. Budgets flex in real time. Campaigns can be paused, pivoted, or amplified mid-flight based on performance data.

🎯
Enhanced targeting precision

Advanced targeting options let teams focus spend on exact demographics, locations, or behaviours — minimising waste and maximising relevance for each audience segment.

💰
Cost-effective vs. traditional

Compared to print, TV, or radio, social campaigns offer a lower floor for entry and more granular budget control — critical for teams running multiple simultaneous campaigns.

🚀
Viral amplification potential

Shareable, resonant content can spread organically well beyond paid reach — exponentially increasing brand visibility without proportional spend increases.

03  ·  Research

Two types of tools. One shared gap.

Competitor analysis in social media campaigns means assessing rivals' strategies, content creation flows, and scheduling experiences to find where each tool breaks down — and where our opportunity sat.

1
Campaign-level visibility was missing everywhere

Every tool treated posts as individual items. No tool connected related posts into a campaign unit with shared status, goal, and performance view.

2
Simplicity vs. depth was a false trade-off

Simple tools were fast but shallow. Deep tools were powerful but slow. The gap was a creation flow that starts simple and reveals depth only when needed.

3
Multi-platform publishing was universally clunky

Managing the same campaign across Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, and Twitter required repetitive manual steps in every tool. Automation was either absent or unreliable.

04  ·  UX Strategy

Three personas. Three different definitions of success.

Understanding the unique goals of each user type shaped every structural decision in the product. The tool had to serve a solo marketer's speed, a manager's oversight, and an executive's need for proof of impact.

05  ·  Ideation

Sketching before committing.

Early sketches explored the core question: how do you structure a creation flow that's fast for experts but doesn't lose beginners? The sketch phase explored linear vs. modular approaches before landing on the step-by-step pattern that made it into wires.

06  ·  Wireframes

Structure before polish.

Wireframes locked the information hierarchy and interaction model before any visual work started. Key decisions around the step indicator, platform selection, and post preview panel were made and validated at this stage.

Low-fidelity wireframes showing the step-by-step campaign creation flow, platform toggle pattern, and preview layout. Validated with the PM and engineers before high-fidelity work began.

1
Step indicator up top

A persistent step indicator at the top of the creation modal showed users exactly where they were and how many steps remained — reducing abandonment anxiety.

2
Platform toggle, not separate forms

Instead of a separate form per platform, a toggle switched the preview pane to show how the same content would render on each channel. One input, multiple previews.

3
Live preview as a trust signal

Showing the post preview as users typed removed uncertainty. Teams stopped asking "how will this look?" and focused on whether the content was right.

07  ·  Design

Four iterations. One final solution.

Each iteration incorporated user feedback, stakeholder input, and engineering constraints. The progression moved from a functional but noisy first version to a clean, focused final experience where every element earns its place on the screen.

Iteration 1 — First pass
All elements on one screen. Feedback: felt overwhelming. The platform selector, content area, and schedule controls competed for attention simultaneously.
Iteration 2 — Phased reveal
Introduced the step-by-step wizard model. Reduced cognitive load significantly. Feedback: step transitions felt abrupt and the back-navigation was unclear.
Iteration 3 — Refined transitions
Smoothed step transitions. Added persistent step indicator and progress bar. Preview panel introduced alongside content step. Feedback: preview needed to be larger.
Iteration 4 — Preview-first layout
Split layout: form on left, live preview on right. Platform toggle drives the preview pane. Nearly final — minor copy and spacing refinements remained.
Final solution
Final — Shipped design
The final campaign creation experience
Step-by-step creation with live preview. Platform toggle, smart scheduling, and a review step before publish. Approved at design review and handed off to engineering in week 3.
08  ·  User Story

Prem, Digital Marketing Manager at Dunzo Daily.

Prem wants to run a targeted social media campaign to increase brand awareness and drive customer engagement for Dunzo Daily. He needs a platform that lets him:

  • Create and schedule captivating posts without needing a separate design tool
  • Track campaign performance metrics across platforms in one dashboard
  • Manage multiple active campaigns without losing context between them
  • Get brand manager approval without resorting to email or Slack threads
  • Hand off campaign reports to his director without manual data assembly

The outcome

By using the redesigned campaign tool, Prem launches his campaign in under 20 minutes — down from a 2-hour process involving three separate tools. His director sees performance in one click. No exports. No spreadsheets.

09  ·  Edge Cases & Error States

What happens when things go wrong.

A creation flow is only as good as its error handling. Designing for edge cases — failed uploads, scheduling conflicts, platform API errors, and incomplete drafts — prevented the majority of support tickets after launch.

Upload failures

File too large, unsupported format, or API timeout. Each error surfaces a specific, actionable message — not a generic "something went wrong." Users know exactly what to fix.

Scheduling conflicts

When two posts are scheduled too close together on the same platform, the system flags the conflict before save — not after publish — with a one-click reschedule option.

Draft preservation

Closing the creation modal mid-flow saves a draft automatically. Users return to exactly where they left off — no progress lost, no confirmation dialogs required.

10  ·  Learnings

What this project taught me.

1
Social platforms change. Design for adaptability.

Platform specs, character limits, and media requirements shift regularly. Building the platform-specific logic as a configurable layer — not hardcoded — meant we could update for Twitter's changes without a full redeploy.

2
User feedback beats assumptions, every time

Iteration 1 felt right in Figma. Two sessions with actual marketers surfaced the cognitive load problem the whole team had missed. Build in testing time even on tight timelines — it always pays back.

3
The wizard pattern works — until it doesn't

Step-by-step flows reduce overwhelm for new users but frustrate power users who know exactly what they need. The next iteration should let experienced users skip to any step and collapse completed sections.
